    Fit your simulator to the real world

    Show Tether how something actually slides on your table: a phone video, or the push log your robot already writes. You get the physics that differs from your simulator, how sure that is, which pushes would settle the rest, and a config for NVIDIA Newton or Isaac Lab.

    In 30 seconds

    1. Robots practise in a virtual world. The real table is a bit different (slipperier, a wet patch), so for real they keep missing.
    2. Give it a record of real pushes. A phone video or a robot log. No data? Make a virtual table below.
    3. The AI finds what is different, fixes the virtual world, and tells you which push to try next.

    No video at hand? Simulate your table

    Set the physics of the "real" table, and NVIDIA Newton produces the measurements: a phone-style video it renders, or a robot's push log. Tether then has to work out your settings from the data alone; you see how close it got at the end.

    Or open a recorded example

    Film your own table

    1. Lay a sheet of A4 or Letter paper flat on the table, beside the path.
    2. Hold the phone still (or prop it up) so the sheet and the whole path are in view.
    3. Flick the object 6–10 times from the same spot, short and long. Let it stop each time.
    4. Use slow motion (120 fps) or 60 fps if your phone has it: motion blur at 30 fps makes launch speeds read low.

    Or upload a robot log

    CSV or JSON, one row per push. stop in metres and a command (or measured launch_speed) are required; target, perceived and a track (positions at 30 fps, space separated) let it separate actuator, friction and camera errors.

    Find what differs

    An agent works through your measurements like a scientist: inspect the evidence, propose which physical effects exist, fit each candidate, and ask for more pushes where the data can't decide. The numbers always come from a least-squares fit and a bootstrap over your pushes, never from the language model.
